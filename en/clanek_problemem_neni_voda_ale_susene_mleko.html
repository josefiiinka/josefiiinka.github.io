The problem isn't the water, but the powdered formula

Tap water from the public water supply in the Czech Republic is generally of such good quality that the water quality alone does not make boiling necessary, even when we want to prepare a supplementary feed of powdered infant formula.

That is, of course, provided we don't live in an older house with unsuitable plumbing, and there hasn't been a water-main incident or a notice about deteriorating water quality. If we use water from a private well, we need an up-to-date test confirming that it is suitable for infants too.

But that doesn't mean we can necessarily skip boiling the water when preparing infant formula. The powder itself matters as well.

Intuitively, it might seem enough to warm the water to about 40 °C, add the powder and feed the baby.

Years ago, I asked a friend who had already been giving her child supplementary formula for several years. She told me: “I'm constantly boiling water. Sometimes ten times over. Even when we travel, I bring my own boiled water in containers.”

If I hadn't checked the information behind her advice, I think I would have boiled my own brain with two small children to look after.

Paradoxically, the problem lies elsewhere

Powdered infant formula is not sterile. Even an unopened package can contain Cronobacter sakazakii, a bacterium that can survive for long periods in dry conditions.

Infections caused by this bacterium are rare, but they can lead to serious complications in very young infants, including sepsis or meningitis. Babies under two months old, premature babies and those with weakened immune systems are most at risk.

And this brings us to an interesting paradox. If we boil the water and then cool it to 40 °C, we eliminate potential microorganisms in the water. But adding the formula afterwards will not kill any bacteria that may be present in the powder.

The World Health Organization (WHO) therefore recommends preparing standard powdered infant formula with water at a temperature of at least 70 °C. This significantly reduces the risk from bacterial contamination. The prepared formula must then be cooled to a suitable drinking temperature.

This doesn't mean every package of powdered formula contains harmful bacteria. Contamination is rare, and preparing formula at 40 °C does not automatically mean a baby will develop an infection.

What about the beneficial ingredients in formula?

To make things more complicated, hot water has its drawbacks too.

Some infant formulas contain added probiotics: live microorganisms that may be killed if the water is too hot. Heat can also affect some vitamins and other sensitive ingredients.

That does not mean standard infant formula prepared at 70 °C loses its nutritional value.

While WHO recommends water at a temperature of at least 70 °C for standard powdered infant formula, some manufacturers specify temperatures of around 40 °C on their packaging. For specialised products containing heat-sensitive ingredients, a lower temperature may be necessary.

Nor do all health authorities recommend the same method. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) allows powdered formula for most healthy infants to be prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions, provided safe water is used. It recommends additional precautions for the youngest and medically vulnerable babies.

When choosing how to prepare formula, it is therefore important to consider the specific product, the baby's age and their health.

So how can we prepare formula safely?

When following WHO guidance, we boil fresh drinking water and let it cool, but not below 70 °C. Only then do we add the measured amount of powdered formula.

We then cool the prepared feed to a suitable drinking temperature, for example under cold running water. Before feeding, we check that it isn't too hot.

Hand hygiene, thorough cleaning and sterilising of bottles and teats, correct measurements and safe storage also matter.

Prepared formula should be used within two hours of preparation and within one hour of the start of feeding. If it won't be used straight away, it should go directly into the fridge and be used within 24 hours. Leftover formula from a feed should be discarded.

For the most vulnerable infants, ready-to-feed liquid formula, which is manufactured to be sterile, may be a more suitable option.

Supplementary formula feeding isn't always as simple as it may seem, especially when health authorities' recommendations and manufacturers' instructions can differ.

One thing is useful to understand: boiling addresses possible microbial contamination in the water. Using water hot enough when mixing it with the powder reduces the risk from bacteria in the formula itself.
